# Abstract

Why did the fossil economy begin in Britain rather than in the Netherlands, with Europe's densest waterways, or China, with coal and rivers in abundance? Terje Tvedt's answer, in this journal, was that Britain alone could engineer its water to deliver coal cheaply: water was the precondition for a coal economy. That is a claim about sequence, and Britain is where the annual series to test it exist together. Using British sectoral output for 1700–1870, a new canal-mileage series, installed horsepower and the Google Books corpus, we find two growth regimes: an aggregate acceleration from 1775–1792, as the canals were built, absorbed by population, and a per-capita acceleration from 1818 that belongs to steam. Canal mileage predicts coal output, not income. At the Maddison benchmarks the Dutch, Belgian, Chinese and Indian records are consistent with the rule that neither engineered water nor coal was sufficient alone.

**Keywords:** Industrial Revolution, canals, coal, water infrastructure, Great Divergence, Netherlands, China, energy transition, growth regimes
